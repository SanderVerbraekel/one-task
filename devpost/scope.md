# One Task

A working name. A to-do app for ADHD focus swings: you only ever see one task, and the app picks the next one from how focused you are right now.

## The Unique Kernel
You never see the pool and you cannot switch tasks. The app shows one task, and it chooses that task from your focus score and how much effort the task takes. Finish it, your focus updates, and only then does the next task appear.

## Who It's For
Someone with ADHD, like the learner, whose focus goes through highs and lows. They are trying to reach goals by doing tasks they can actually manage, and by finishing what they started, instead of facing a whole list and hopping between items.

## The Core Loop
They add tasks into a hidden pool. For each task they decide, with their own judgment, whether it is low effort or high effort, and they enter how long they think it will take. The app shows exactly one task: its detail, whether it is low or high effort, and the focus score. They go do the task (write the mail), come back, and press "Task finished." Time spent versus the estimate updates the focus score — more time over the estimate means less focus. The next task is one they can handle at that score. The score changes after every task, because ADHD focus is not steady. They come back because the next manageable task is waiting, and the pile stays out of sight.

## Inspiration & Identity
A simple screen: one task, its detail, low or high effort, a focus meter, and a button to add a task. No other look-and-feel references yet.

## Why This Matters to the Learner
"It would help me finally reach my goals by giving me tasks I can manage when on low or high focus and finish what I started."

## What "Working" Looks Like
In a short screen recording: a task such as "write a mail" is the only thing on screen, with its effort and a time estimate entered ahead of time. They finish the real task away from the app, come back, and press "Task finished." The focus score changes based on time spent versus that estimate, and the next task that appears matches the new score — a lighter task after running long, a heavier one when they stayed within the estimate. That update, and the task that follows it, is the moment that proves it.

## The POC Boundary
In: add a task with a self-judged effort (low or high) and a time estimate; keep every other task hidden; show one task with its detail, effort, and focus score; no switching; "Task finished"; compare time spent with the estimate and update focus (over the estimate lowers focus); pick the next task for the effort the current score can handle; show the updated score after each task.

## Later
A Pomodoro timer as its own way to judge focus. It was an example of the same idea; the proof uses time spent versus the estimate after "Task finished."

Setting the focus score by hand. The demo that matters is the score changing from the task they just finished.

## Explicitly Cut
Seeing or browsing the task pool. The kernel is that the list stays hidden.

Switching to another task before the current one is finished. They said you can't just switch.

Doing the real-world task inside the app. Writing the mail happens away from the app; the app only receives "Task finished."

The app assigning effort. They choose low or high effort themselves when they create the task.
